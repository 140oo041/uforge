Four directions for the inspector

Every mockup shows the same selection — router R1, weighted arbitration, three attached components, one trunk — so the differences you see are the design, not the data. All four keep the VS Code webview theme variables and the existing edit-intent wiring; this is a restyle of the panel, not a rework of the model.

A Aligned form B Grouped cards C Tabbed, no scroll D Numbers first

Direction A · conservative

Aligned form

The current panel, disciplined. Same single scroll, but every control sits on a strict label column, units live next to values, long notes collapse into hover hints, and destructive actions leave the flow.

What changed

  • Identity header — glyph, name, and a one-line summary (“3 attached · 1 trunk”) replace the bare h3, so the selection reads instantly.
  • Strict label column + units. Inputs align on one edge and every number says what it is (“cy / hop”, “pkt / port·cy”) — today that knowledge hides in tooltips.
  • Notes become hints. The long explanatory paragraphs become a dotted ? that reveals on hover. Section rules carry the count and the “open source” link.
  • Danger exits the flow. Delete moves under the ⋯ menu instead of a red button that scrolls into view under every selection.
  • Model usage shown — each latency model says how many wires ride it, which the graph already knows.

Trade-off

  • Lowest effort and least visual novelty — the panel stays one long scroll, so a router with many attachments still buries the trunks.

Direction B · structured

Grouped cards

Each concern becomes a collapsible card under a sticky header. The panel stops being a wall of rows: you fold away what you're not tuning, and the header (with the rename field and quick actions) never scrolls out from under you.

What changed

  • Cards with fold state. Forwarding, Attached, Trunks, and Models are separate surfaces. Collapsed state can persist per kind — tune weights all day without scrolling past queue settings.
  • Sticky header. Rename-in-place on the title, quick actions as an icon row, delete isolated at the far end in its own hover color.
  • Summaries carry data. A folded card still shows its payload (“1 cy”, “3”) — closed doesn't mean invisible.
  • Steppers for weights — the most-touched number on a weighted router becomes ±1 clicks instead of a blur-committed text field.
  • One hint per card, in a consistent ⓘ footer slot, replacing scattered ins-note paragraphs.

Trade-off

  • Cards cost ~24px each in chrome; on a small panel with everything expanded you see slightly less at once than direction A.

Direction C · flat panel

Tabbed, no scroll

The inspector becomes three shallow tabs with a stat line in the header and a pinned action bar. Nothing important lives below the fold, ever. Try the tabs — they work.

What changed

  • Stat line as the summary. The four numbers you check most sit in the header in monospace — visible from any tab, no scanning rows.
  • Breadcrumb path replaces the path row and doubles as drill-out navigation for nested composites.
  • Labels above full-width controls (VS Code settings idiom) — selects stop truncating their option text at narrow panel widths.
  • Pinned footer. Open source and Delete are always reachable, and Delete stops visually colliding with per-row ✕ buttons.
  • Maps to every selection: blocks get Params / Ports / Vars, wires get Route / Latency, traffic gens get Traffic / Ports.

Trade-off

  • Tabs hide two-thirds of the state — cross-referencing weight against queue bound now takes a click. Wrong choice if users compare across sections often.

Direction D · opinionated

Numbers first

An inspector for people who think in cycles and packets. The scalar params become a stat-tile grid you click to edit; lists compress into tables and toggle chips. Densest of the four — closer to a scope readout than a form.

What changed

  • Stat tiles. The numbers are the interface: big, monospace, tabular, with units. Click a tile → it becomes an input; Enter commits through the same edit intent.
  • Derived data for free. The weight table shows each component's effective bandwidth share (60/20/20) — the thing weighted arbitration is actually about, computed nowhere in the current UI.
  • Trunks as toggle chips — one tap on/off, matching how the canvas already draws router↔router adjacency, plus an inline “+ router”.
  • Model chips carry usage (×2 wires); unused ones dim.
  • Scales to the run. After a sim, tiles can flip to showing live occupancy / drops next to the configured bound — config and result in one readout.

Trade-off

  • Click-to-edit is a less obvious affordance than a visible input, and the density is unapologetic — this direction bets your users are hardware people who prefer a scope face to a settings form.

Details worth keeping, whichever direction wins

These four decisions showed up in every sketch that worked — they're independent of the overall structure and could land as a first incremental PR.

Status as chips with dots

wired / inferred / unresolved become colored-dot chips (green · grey · red) reused identically on wires, ports, and the design summary — one vocabulary everywhere.

Units on every number

latency cy/hop, bandwidth pkt/port·cy, queue pkt. The tooltips already know this; the panel should say it.

Danger out of the flow

Delete never sits as a big red block in the scroll. Overflow menu (A), isolated icon (B), or pinned footer (C) — any of them beats today's placement.

Notes → hints

Every ins-note paragraph becomes a hover/expand affordance in a consistent slot. First-time users still get the explanation; returning users get their rows back.

Recommendation

B is the strongest base: collapsible cards fix the real problem (one undifferentiated scroll) without hiding state behind tabs, and the sticky header + isolated delete are straight wins. It also degrades gracefully — a wire's panel is just two cards.

Steal from D: the stat-tile row for router numerics and the derived bandwidth-share column. A “Forwarding” card whose header row is the tile strip gives you B's structure with D's readability, and sets up the config-vs-measured story once a trace is loaded.
